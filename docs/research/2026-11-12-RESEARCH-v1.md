# Linear Types × Actors × Transactions: A Survey of What Has Been Built, What Broke, and What It Means for a Unified `Txn` Model

The closest existing realisation of "linear resources + actors + atomic multi-resource transactions" is Microsoft Project Verona's Behaviour-Oriented Concurrency (BoC): it pairs a region/ownership type system with `when (a, b, …) { … }` behaviours that atomically and deadlock-freely acquire several isolated "cowns", and it does so *without* rollback. That design choice dodges the IO-in-transactions problem that killed STM.NET and Akka Transactors, but at the cost of requiring the footprint to be declared up front. Nothing in the literature unifies linear types, actors and *user-extensible, rollback-capable* transactions in one typed model. Your `Txn (T 1)` idea therefore sits in a real gap, and the prior art below tells you fairly precisely where the sharp edges are.

## TL;DR

- **The proven pattern is "ownership for single-owner correctness + pessimistic, declared-footprint acquisition for cross-actor atomicity".** Verona/BoC, Sing#, Pony and Durable Functions critical sections all follow it. Every optimistic STM bolted onto actors (Akka Transactors, STM.NET, Orleans' first snapshot-isolation design) was removed or abandoned. The reasons given were IO irrevocability, scalability/distribution and user-facing complexity.
- **Rollback over linear resources is the unsolved core.** Haskell STM bans IO by type. `unsafeIOToSTM` is documented as "highly dangerous". Orleans leaves side-effect-freedom of transactional methods "to programmer discipline". I found no peer-reviewed paper that uses linear or uniqueness types to admit irrevocable resources inside transactions. The workable building blocks are transactional boosting (inverse operations + abstract locks), open nesting (compensation handlers), ownership-aware commit, sagas, and irrevocable-mode transactions.
- **Numbers to anchor expectations.** BoC beats Pony on 17 of 22 Savina benchmarks when used actor-style. It is more than 100× faster on 1 core (more than 35× on 8 cores) on a multi-account atomic-transfer benchmark, using 35% of the code. Orleans distributed ACID transactions run at 46 KTPS (single grain) and 11 KTPS (two grains), against 430/212 KTPS for plain writes on one silo. Rust's ownership model, the most widely deployed linear-ish system, was described by one participant in Fulton et al.'s SOUPS 2021 study as having "a near-vertical learning curve".

## Key Findings

1. **Linearity gives you safe single-owner use and zero-copy transfer, not multi-party atomicity.** This is exactly your observation. Pony, Sing# and LaCasa all stop at "isolation + transfer". Verona adds BoC on top precisely because "actor model programming has a serious pain point: updating multiple actors as a single atomic operation is a challenging task."\[1\]
2. **Pessimistic, footprint-declared atomicity (BoC, Durable Functions, deterministic DBs) composes with linear resources.** The reason is that nothing is ever re-executed. Optimistic re-execution does not compose with them without an undo story.
3. **Extensibility is necessary but dangerous.** Every system that lets users define conflict and rollback semantics (boosting, open nesting, Orleans storage plugins) moves the correctness burden onto the user. In practice the user must supply abstract locks, inverses/compensations and commutativity specifications. Some objects, such as a singly-linked FIFO queue, have "no obvious inverse."\[2\]
4. **Users hate invisible locking.** Orleans users were surprised by deadlock-timeout aborts "since locking is not explicit in the programming model". Durable Functions instead constrains critical sections syntactically to guarantee deadlock freedom.\[3\]

## 1. Linear / Affine / Uniqueness Types + Actors / Message Passing

### Pony (Clebsch, Drossopoulou, Blessing, McNeil — "Deny Capabilities for Safe, Fast Actors", AGERE! 2015)

- **What it is.** An actor language whose six reference capabilities (`iso`, `trn`, `ref`, `val`, `box`, `tag`) are defined by which aliases they *deny* locally and globally.\[4\]
- **Key design.** Only `iso`, `val` and `tag` are sendable. `iso` is moved by `consume` or destructive read, so a mutable object graph crosses actors by pointer with no copy.\[5\]\[6\] `recover` blocks lift `ref` to `iso` or `val`. Actors are typed `tag` from outside, so they offer identity plus async messaging only. Defaults (`tag` for actors, `ref` for classes, `val` for primitives) and local inference reduce annotation load.\[7\]
- **Issues.** Pony gives no multi-actor atomicity. In the BoC paper's "ReasonableBanking" experiment, achieving atomic, ordered multi-account transfers in Pony required a hand-written Teller/Account/Manager acquire–stash–commit protocol that "requires the actors … to be aware of the protocol, thus mixing business logic with the protocol". It came to 226 LoC, versus 78 LoC in BoC.\[1\]
- **Stats.** BoC's authors chose Pony as the baseline because the most recent Savina comparison found Pony comparable to Akka and CAF. Pony lost on benchmarks where messages carry actor references (Banking, Chameneos, Concurrent Dictionary). There, "memory management costs" of Pony's remembered-set/tracing GC caused the overhead, compared with BoC-in-C++'s reference counting.\[1\]

### Singularity OS / Sing# (Fähndrich, Aiken, Hawblitzel, Hodson, Hunt, Larus, Levi — EuroSys 2006; Hunt & Larus, OSR 2007)

- **What it is.** An OS whose software-isolated processes (SIPs) communicate only over typed channels. Message payloads live in a separate *exchange heap* governed by a linear ownership discipline.\[8\]\[9\]\[10\]
- **Key design.** "Each block in the exchange heap has at most one owning thread at any point in time."\[11\] Channel contracts are state machines checked by the compiler. Verification proves "1) processes only access memory they own, 2) processes never leak a block of memory, and 3) send and receive operations on channels are never applied in the wrong protocol state." Ownership is handed over through `[Claims]` parameters, and errors appear at compile time, e.g. "Leaking owned reference". The authors note that "our resource tracking is rather similar to linear types and can be formalized using separation logic." Ownership is verified twice: once on source and once on compiled MSIL.\[8\]\[12\]
- **Issues.** "We do not check exceptional program paths."\[8\] This is the same gap that bites every linear system with exceptions (see Exceptional GV below). Later formalisation work ("Typing Copyless Message Passing") showed that a Sing#-style function can leak memory if it sends an endpoint over its own peer channel. The linear discipline therefore needed refinement.\[13\]
- **Stats.** After 8 months in use, the codebase relied on 43 channel contracts with about 500 distinct messages and 135 protocol states. Of those messages, 146 carry exchange-heap pointers and 42 carry channel endpoints, in a system of "roughly 300K lines of commented Sing# code" (Fähndrich et al., EuroSys 2006). A two-thread message ping-pong costs 2,462 cycles, against 10,758 on Linux and 12,806 on Windows (AMD Athlon 64). Channels "outperform the mechanisms available on other systems by factors of 4 to 5", and send–receive cost stays at about 1,200 cycles from 1 byte to 64 KB.
- **Relevance to you.** This is the most direct precedent for an *actor OS with linear message ownership and protocol-typed channels*. It had no transactional layer.

### Verona's region/reference-capability system (Arvidsson et al., "Reference Capabilities for Flexible Memory Management", OOPSLA 2023)

- **Key design.** Regions with unique entry points (`iso`). A *cown* "is a wrapper around an iso that permits regions to be indirectly shared across multiple threads of control, but importantly does not permit direct access to its contents." For an `iso`, access is synchronous because exclusivity is already established. For a cown, "access is asynchronous and will only commence after exclusive access has been established dynamically."\[14\]\[15\] This separation of *static uniqueness* and *dynamic exclusivity acquisition* is a clean template for `T 1` versus `Txn (T 1)`.

### LaCasa (Haller & Loiko, OOPSLA 2016)

- **What it is.** A Scala compiler plugin adding affine "boxes" plus the object-capability discipline. It gives unique references for by-reference actor messaging with "deep-copy" semantics.\[16\]
- **Key design.** It uses the ocap property ("whether a class is ocap is just one bit of information") to minimise annotations. It also leans on Scala implicits and path-dependent types to keep permissions affine.\[17\]
- **Stats.** The authors evaluated how well existing Scala open-source code already conforms to the ocap restrictions, on a corpus of over 75,000 LOC.\[18\] This is one of the few adoption-cost measurements for retrofitting affinity onto an actor language.

### Session-typed functional languages: Links / Exceptional GV (Fowler, Lindley, Morris, Decova — POPL 2019)

- **What it is.** The first integration of asynchronous session types with exception handling in a linear λ-calculus, implemented in Links.\[19\]
- **Key lesson.** The paper states bluntly: "In practice, linear session types are unrealistic". Real programs involve exceptions and disconnection, so linearity must be relaxed or paired with cancellation and failure propagation.\[19\]\[20\] This is directly analogous to your rollback problem: what happens to an un-consumed linear endpoint when a computation aborts?
- **Related.** Work on deadlock-free separation logic (Iris/Actris) shows that *affinity* (being allowed to drop) re-admits deadlocks. A program that drops a channel without sending can block its peer forever. Full linearity is what yields progress.\[21\] **Implication:** if `Txn` aborts must "drop" a linear handle, you need an explicit cancel/close protocol rather than silent affinity.

### Other systems in this space (brief pointers)

- **Encore** (Castegren & Wrigstad, "Reference capabilities for concurrency control", ECOOP 2016): active objects with capability types.\[22\]
- **Kilim**: isolation types for Java actors.
- **Rust actor frameworks**: Actix and others, which move `Send` values into mailboxes.
- **Clean** (uniqueness types for IO).
- **Mezzo**, **Vale**, **Austral**, **Granule**, **Alms**, **ATS** and **Linear Haskell** (`-XLinearTypes`): these explore similar ownership/linearity ground. In the sources I gathered, none of them ships a transactional layer integrated with its linearity discipline.

## 2. Linear / Affine Types + Transactions / STM

### GHC STM (Harris, Marlow, Peyton Jones, Herlihy — "Composable Memory Transactions", PPoPP 2005)

- **Key design.** `atomically :: STM a -> IO a`, `retry :: STM a`, `orElse :: STM a -> STM a -> STM a`. "Splitting the world into STM actions and I/O actions" statically prevents `launchMissiles` inside a transaction. `retry` blocks the thread until a TVar it read changes. `orElse` "requires the underlying STM implementation to support genuine nested transactions." Exceptions abort the transaction with no effect.\[23\]
- **The escape hatch and its caveats.** GHC's `unsafeIOToSTM` is documented as "a highly dangerous thing to do". The documentation warns that "the STM implementation will often run transactions multiple times", that "the transaction may have seen an inconsistent view of memory when the IO runs", and that you must not "acquire any resources that need releasing (exception handlers are ignored when aborting the transaction)".\[24\]\[25\] **For a linear FileHandle this is the crux.** An aborted transaction can neither release nor return the resource, which is exactly the hole a linear `Txn` must plug.
- **Workaround research.** Schröder's diploma thesis "Durability and Contention in STM" adds `atomicallyWithIO` with I/O *finalizers* that run at commit.\[26\] This is the "defer irrevocable effects to commit" pattern; it has no linear types.

### Ownership-aware TM (Agrawal, Lee, Sukha — "Safe Open-Nested Transactions Through Ownership", PPoPP 2009)

- **What it is.** The clearest paper combining *ownership types* with TM semantics.
- **Key design.** Modules form a tree. "An ownership-aware commit of a transaction T commits a memory location globally if that location belongs to the module of T; otherwise, the read or write to the location is propagated up to T's parent transaction." The authors prove "serializability by modules" (multilevel serializability adapted to TM), and the type system enforces most constraints statically. Corollary: "open-nested transactions are serializable when modules do not share data."\[27\]
- **Issues.** It is "still a mechanism; programmers must still use it in combination with abstract locks and compensating actions." It is also free of semantic deadlock only if aborting transactions obey memory-footprint restrictions.\[27\]
- **Relevance.** This is the best formal precedent for "ownership decides what commits where". Map module ownership onto actor ownership and you get "an actor's own state commits locally; foreign state propagates to the enclosing transaction."

### Typestate + atomic blocks (Beckman, Bierhoff, Aldrich — "Verifying Correct Usage of Atomic Blocks and Typestate", OOPSLA 2008)

- **Key design.** This work combines *access permissions* (linear-logic-style unique/full/share/pure/immutable) with atomic blocks. It reinterprets permissions "as an approximation of the thread-sharedness of a location in memory." It claims "the first work that statically verifies the proper placement of atomic blocks in object-oriented code."\[28\]
- **Relevance.** It directly supports your "linearity covers single-owner use; atomicity is needed only where permissions are shared" split. A unique permission needs no transaction, while a shared permission does.

### Irrevocability

- **Irrevocable transactions** (Welc, Saha, Adl-Tabatabai, SPAA 2008) let a transaction perform non-undoable actions by guaranteeing it never aborts. They are restricted to at most one irrevocable transaction at a time, which "reduc[es] parallelism" (per the BoC paper's summary).\[1\]\[29\]\[30\]
- **xCalls** (Volos et al., EuroSys 2009) measured that "of 284 Linux system calls… only 33 require irrevocable transactions". Most can be made transactional with deferral or compensation.\[31\] This is useful evidence that a hook-based `Txn` can cover most IO with a small irrevocable residue.
- **C++ TM TS (N3725)** uses `transaction_safe` / `transaction_unsafe` attributes.\[32\] That is an *effect annotation*, not linearity, and it is the closest mainstream analogue of "effect systems controlling what may run in a transaction."

### Rust STM

- The `stm` crate (modelled on Haskell's) tells users: "Don't have side effects except for the atomic variables… Especially a mutex or other blocking mechanisms inside of software transactional memory is dangerous",\[33\] and "calls to `atomically` should not be nested."\[34\] Rust has no purity/effect type, so the Haskell guarantee degrades to documentation.
- Community discussion on the Rust users forum names the missing piece explicitly: STM wants the compiler to prove a function pure so it can be retried "w/o fear of side effects", and Rust cannot. One practitioner describes an in-memory 2PC design in which "each transaction holds an `&mut T`, which forces all access to T to pass through the transaction" and "only allows operations that can be reliably reversed", with presumed-commit on drop.\[35\] **That is essentially a linear-capability transaction and is the closest folk design to your `Txn (T 1)`.**
- Lively's Harvard senior thesis (2018) ported the STO TM to Rust and found that "many of the mistakes users could make when programming with STO in C++ can be statically prevented using Rust's unique type system". It still "cannot provide the full transactional safety of the Haskell system."\[36\]
- **My read:** Rust STM never took off for three reasons. Affine types without an effect system cannot stop irrevocable side effects in a retried closure. `&mut` exclusivity already removes much of the need. And the async/actor ecosystem standardised on channels and mutexes.

### Gap

I found **no peer-reviewed paper that uses linear or uniqueness types specifically to admit irrevocable I/O or linear resources inside transactions**. The literature uses either type-level exclusion (Haskell), effect annotations (C++ TM), runtime irrevocability (Welc et al.), or commit-time finalizers (Schröder). This is an open niche.

## 3. Actors + Transactions

### Transactors (Field & Varela, POPL 2005)

- **What it is.** "A fault-tolerant programming model for composing loosely-coupled distributed components running in an unreliable environment such as the internet into systems that reliably maintain globally consistent distributed state."\[37\]
- **Key design.** Transactors explicitly model node failures, network failures, persistent storage and state immutability. Messages are still sent to and executed by a single actor (per the BoC paper's comparison).\[1\] Follow-up work: Kuang, Field & Varela, "Fault Tolerant Distributed Computing Using Asynchronous Local Checkpointing" (AGERE! 2014).\[38\]

### Akka Transactors and transactional Agents (removed)

- **What happened.** The Akka 2.3 migration guide states: "The integration between actors and STM in the module akka-transactor is deprecated and will be removed in a future version."\[39\] Transactional Agents were deprecated at the same time.\[40\]
- **Why.** Akka team member Patrik Nordwall: "The main reasons are that it is not scalable and distributable. For local usage you can use STM, but you don't need the Akka abstractions for that."\[41\] Roland Kuhn: "STM is considered as a failed experiment (see for example Microsoft's reasoning for removing it from .Net) and it was never compatible with distributed computing."\[42\] The recommended replacement was "use one actor as the consistency boundary."\[40\]
- **Structural critique (BoC paper).** Transactors "required splitting the world in transactional (STM) state and Actor state". Transactions on shared state must then either run on separate threads, losing ordering, or all run on one thread, losing concurrency.\[1\]

### Orleans ACID transactions (Eldeeb, Burckhardt, Bond, Cidon, Yang, Bernstein — VLDB 2024)

- **Key design.**
  - Opt-in `ITransactionalState<T>` with `PerformRead` / `PerformUpdate`.\[43\]
  - Method attributes: `Create`, `Join`, `CreateOrJoin`.\[43\]
  - 2PL at grain granularity plus 2PC, with no central transaction manager. The coordinator role is assigned to a participant grain.\[43\]
  - Distributed *early lock release* at phase one of 2PC, with commit-dependency tracking and cascading abort.\[44\]
  - *Reconnaissance queries*, which dry-run the transaction to prefetch state and learn the footprint, so locks can be acquired in a global order and deadlock avoided.\[43\]
  - Pluggable `ITransactionalStateStorage` requiring only ETag-conditional writes.\[43\]
- **Issues and experiences.**
  - "Transactional methods should not have any side effects beyond changing the state of transactional grains… This is not enforced by Orleans, and left to programmer discipline."\[43\]
  - Deadlock-timeout aborts "were identified as a major source of performance problems. The possibility of them occurring was often a surprise for users since locking is not explicit in the programming model." Users then set "conservatively short" timeouts, causing many aborts.\[43\]
  - Users rejected a base-class (inheritance) API in favour of composition/dependency injection, and refused extra deployable components.\[43\]
  - Deep-copying whole grain state for rollback is costly, and the authors suggest "a custom transactional variation of the data structure that can log and undo incremental updates" as future work.\[43\]
  - An earlier Orleans design with multi-master replication and snapshot isolation "was dropped before Orleans was released because it performed poorly and users found it too complex."\[43\]
- **Stats** (single silo, in-memory storage, Azure D8as v5):\[43\]

| Operation | Single grain (KTPS) | Two grains (KTPS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plain writes | 430 | 212 |
| Persistent writes | 126 | 61 |
| Transactions | 46 | 11 |

- A cloud-storage write takes about 20 ms, so naive 2PC holds locks about 40 ms longer, limiting hot-data throughput to roughly 25 tps.\[43\]
- Reconnaissance queries are wasteful at low contention but "crucial" at high contention, where they avoided deadlock aborts entirely on SmallBank multi-transfer.\[43\]
- **Earlier data point.** In the 2011 Orleans paper, Chirper throughput was 13% higher with the transaction system disabled, and 2% better with 100 ms of processing time.\[45\]

### Snapper (Liu, Su, Shah, Zhou, Salles — SIGMOD 2022)

- **Key design.** A transaction library on Orleans with two abstractions. *PACTs* pre-declare their actor access sets and run on deterministic schedules that never abort.\[44\] *ACTs* are nondeterministic (S2PL). Both can run in hybrid mode.\[44\]\[46\]
- **Stats.** PACTs achieve "up to 2x higher throughput" than Orleans Transactions. A later system, HDCC (VLDB 2025), reports up to 3.1× over Snapper when all transactions pre-declare read/write sets.\[47\]\[48\]
- **Lesson.** Declaring the footprint up front (as in BoC and Calvin) buys large wins. The Orleans authors note the flip side: deterministic systems "typically have to restrict the programming model to one-shot stored procedures, and need to know the transaction's readset and writeset ahead of time."\[43\]

### Azure Durable Functions entities and critical sections (Burckhardt et al., "Durable Functions: Semantics for Stateful Serverless", OOPSLA 2021)

- **Key design.** Entities are virtual-actor-like and process operations one at a time. Orchestrations use `LockAsync(e1, e2)` to open a critical section. The restrictions are:
  - critical sections cannot be nested;
  - they cannot create sub-orchestrations;
  - they can call only the entities they locked;
  - they can signal only entities they did not lock;
  - they cannot call the same entity in parallel.\[49\]\[50\]

  Entities cannot call entities. The payoff is that critical sections are "guaranteed not to deadlock", whereas "Orleans can deadlock, and deadlocks persist until messages time out." Locks are durable and survive process recycling.\[51\]\[52\]\[53\]
- **Caveats.** Orchestrators must be deterministic, because they are replayed, and activities must be idempotent. Critical sections are not available in the Python, PowerShell or Java SDKs.\[50\]\[52\]
- **Relevance.** This is a production-grade instance of "atomic multi-actor section with a declared lock set + syntactic restrictions instead of rollback".

### Chocola (Swalens, De Koster, De Meuter — TOPLAS 2021; "Transactional Actors", SEPS 2017)

- **What it is.** A Clojure extension unifying futures, actors and STM that "maintains the guarantees of all three models wherever possible, even when they are combined." Transactional actors let actors share memory safely through transactions,\[54\]\[55\] and a message sent inside a transaction is tied to that transaction's outcome.
- **Issues.** The library's own TODOs list "garbage collection of unreachable actors" and note that `commute` "doesn't work as expected in transactional futures". The BoC authors criticise that the programmer must decide per-state whether it is transactional (synchronous) or actor-owned (asynchronous).\[1\]\[56\]

### Other systems (brief pointers)

- **Aeon** (Sang et al.): orders actors in a DAG with *dominator* actors serialising multi-actor events. This creates a single point of contention.\[1\]
- **Erlang Mnesia**, **Dapr/Service Fabric reliable actors**, **Temporal** (durable execution), **Ray**, **CAF**, **Coyote**, **Hydro/Bloom/CALM** and **Unison** are adjacent. In the sources I gathered I found no new transactional-actor data on them, so they are not characterised further here.

## 4. All Three Combined (or Close)

### Verona Behaviour-Oriented Concurrency (Cheeseman, Parkinson, Clebsch, Kogias, Drossopoulou, Chisnall, Wrigstad, Liétar — OOPSLA 2023)

- **What it is.** The single most relevant system for you. The underlying language must "separate the heap into disjoint sets with unique entry points" (ownership/regions). The concurrency layer is `when (c1, …, cn) { body }`, which spawns an asynchronous behaviour.\[1\]
- **Semantics.**
  - A behaviour runs only when all its cowns are available. It acquires them atomically and holds them exclusively until it terminates.\[57\]
  - It "cannot acquire more cowns, nor can it prematurely release any cown it is holding."
  - Happens-before: behaviour b precedes b′ if their cown sets overlap and b was spawned first. Ordering is causal and implicit.\[58\]
  - The model is data-race-free and deadlock-free by construction, because cowns are sorted into a global order and enqueued in two phases on an MCS-queue-lock-derived DAG.\[59\]
  - "Unlike more complex systems, there is no need for additional operations, such as logs, commit, or roll-back."
  - Nested `when`s just spawn new behaviours, unlike nested transactions with their "vast and seemingly unresolved" semantic space.\[1\]
- **Stats** (Azure F72s v2, 72 hardware threads, 100 runs per benchmark):
  - "BoC (Actor)" beats Pony 0.53.0 on **17 of 22** Savina benchmarks.
  - "BoC (Full)", using multi-cown `when`, gave significant speedups on 6 benchmarks, e.g. Dining Philosophers 73.3 → 22.2 ms on 1 core and Sleeping Barber 1,660 → 12.5 ms on 1 core. 9 of the benchmarks also got smaller.
  - ReasonableBanking: BoC takes 78 LoC and 7.9 ms (1 core) / 9.3 ms (8 cores). Pony takes 226 LoC and 987.9 ms / 344.7 ms. BoC is "over 100 times faster than Pony on 1 core, and over 35 times faster … on 8 cores". The authors caution that "in a realistic application, the improvement would be considerably smaller."
  - Dining Philosophers (100 philosophers, 1 ms eat) closely tracks ideal scaling up to the 50-way parallelism limit, beating `std::lock`-based mutexes, whose back-off costs dominate at low thread counts.\[1\]
- **Admitted limitations.**
  1. Dynamically growing resource sets. Acquiring r1 and then conditionally r2 cannot be expressed atomically: STM can "just access more transactional objects", while BoC must release and re-acquire.
  2. No read-only shared acquisition mode.
  3. No promises/return values.
  4. Distribution is open: "we are a long way from making any claims in this space."
  5. Ordering can over-serialise; for example, sequentially scheduled philosophers fully serialise. That is a performance bug but never a correctness bug.\[1\]
- **Ports.** Microsoft has released an official Python package, `microsoft/bocpy`, on PyPI. Tobias Wrigstad, Fridtjof Stoldt and Donghee Na presented it at the Python Language Summit 2026 (reported by Seth Larson on Python Insider, Sept 30 2026): "Programs written with the BOC model are task-based. Tasks are lightweight, can run on any core, and cannot deadlock." The repository notes that bocpy "only achieves true parallelism on Python 3.12+, where each sub-interpreter has its own GIL."
- **Relevance.** BoC is a `Txn` with a fixed, non-extensible policy: pessimistic acquisition with no rollback. That policy is exactly what makes it safe for linear, non-undoable resources. Your extensible `Txn` could treat BoC as its "pessimistic" instance.

### Sing# (revisited)

Sing# delivers linear ownership plus protocol-typed message passing. It has no transactions, and exceptional paths are unchecked.\[8\]

### Reversibility and rollback for actors

- **CauDEr** (Lanese, Nishida, Palacios, Vidal — FLOPS 2018; later distributed extension by Fabbretti, Lanese, Stefani, RC 2021): a causal-consistent reversible debugger for Core Erlang. "Actions can be undone in any order as long as their consequences, if any, are undone first."\[60\]\[61\] It provides `roll` operations that undo a send together with all its causal consequences across processes.\[62\]
- **Checkpoint-based rollback in session programming** (arXiv 2312.02851) and Vidal (2023), "From reversible computation to checkpoint-based rollback recovery for message-passing concurrent programs", carry these semantics from debugging into fault-tolerance rollback.\[63\]
- **Reversible session-based π-calculus** (Tiezzi & Yoshida, 2015).\[63\]
- **Relevance.** Causal-consistent rollback is the right *semantic* notion for cross-actor abort: undo a message and everything it caused. But it presumes all effects are reversible, which a linear external resource is not.

## 5. Extensible / User-Defined Transaction Semantics

### Transactional boosting (Herlihy & Koskinen — PPoPP 2008)

- **Key design.** Forgo read/write sets. Wrap a linearizable object, and for each method the user supplies an *abstract lock* (conflict iff the methods don't commute) and an *inverse* that is logged for rollback. "As long as the linearizable implementation satisfies certain regularity properties (informally, that every method has an inverse)", concurrent transactions without inherent conflicts "synchronize at the same granularity as the original linearizable implementation."\[64\]\[65\]
- **Limits.** Boosting is "intrinsically lock-based, and is not fully general: operations on a single-linked FIFO queue, for example, have no obvious inverse."\[2\] Follow-ups:
  - *Optimistic Transactional Boosting* (Hassan et al., PPoPP 2014) "avoids the need for defining inverse operations".\[64\]
  - *Checkpoints and Continuations instead of Nested Transactions* (Koskinen & Herlihy) rolls back "operationally" with captured continuations and inverses.\[66\]
  - Distributed boosting (Feldman & Herlihy).\[67\]
  - A Scala-STM boosting library that used Scala STM's rollback/completion handlers.\[68\]

### Open nesting (Ni, Menon, Adl-Tabatabai, Hosking, Hudson, Moss, Saha, Shpeisman — PPoPP 2007)

- **Key design.** An open-nested child commits immediately and globally. The user provides abstract locks (two-phase, with hierarchical lock modes) and handlers (`onabort` compensation, `oncommit`, `ontopcommit`).\[69\]\[70\]\[71\]
- **Issues.** "A TM system that permits open nesting cannot guarantee" plain serializability. With open nesting, "the programmer must specify conflicts (via abstract locks) and provide compensation actions (via handlers)." A later analysis says that "in full generality, open-nested transactions do not have clear semantics."\[69\]\[72\] Agrawal et al.'s ownership-aware commit is the fix that restores serializability-by-modules.

### Semantic locking for collections

- Carlstrom et al., "Transactional Collection Classes" (PPoPP 2007), builds multi-level transactions with semantic locks taken in open-nested transactions. Its finding is that open nesting "is probably the most significant requirement" for avoiding spurious memory conflicts in collections.\[73\]
- Haskell's `stm-containers` adapts HAMTs for STM to cut conflict granularity.\[74\]

### Pluggable semantics in production

- Orleans's `ITransactionalStateStorage` is a user-implementable durability hook. Its TM role (prepare/commit/abort) is injected, and its "unit of access" is the whole grain.\[43\]
- The STM.NET team explored Haskell-style `onCommit`/`onRollback` lambdas, per Joe Duffy.\[75\]

### Interaction with linear resources (synthesis)

Boosting's "inverse" and open nesting's "compensation" are the two ways a *non-memory* effect can participate in rollback. A linear resource adds a third requirement: on abort, the resource token must be *returned* to its owner, or explicitly closed. It cannot be dropped. None of the above systems type-checks that obligation.

## 6. Practical Experience Reports and Empirical Data

### STM.NET (Microsoft)

- STM.NET began public life on DevLabs on July 27, 2009 and was cancelled on May 11, 2010.\[76\]
- Joe Duffy's retrospective (January 3, 2010) cites these problems:
  - **The I/O problem.** Most I/O is inherently non-transactional, including file access, event logs, web service calls, user interaction and P/Invoke.\[77\]
  - **Weak atomicity.** Mixed transactional and non-transactional access to the same memory, with object-level buffering causing false sharing. "We direly needed a memory model for TM."\[75\]
  - **Uncertain calling context.** Library code "will not necessarily know whether this method will get called from a transaction", so it must be prepared for transactional access.\[75\]
  - **No killer app.** "We searched and searched for the killer TM app… I became less and less convinced that the killer concurrency apps … needed TM."\[77\]
- Duffy's later Midori work went the isolation/ownership route instead.

### Intel TSX (hardware TM)

- In August 2014, an erratum led Intel to disable TSX on Haswell and early Broadwell via microcode.\[78\]
- The October 2018 microcode disabled the HLE prefix and forced RTM aborts in SGX and SMM modes.\[79\]
- The 2021.1 IPU disabled TSX by default on Skylake through Coffee Lake/Whiskey Lake client parts, and the February 2022 update extended de-featuring to more models.\[80\]\[81\]
- **Lesson.** Even hardware TM ended up as a best-effort fast path, not a semantic foundation.

### Rust ownership usability

- **Fulton et al. (SOUPS 2021)** (Fulton, Chan, Votipka, Hicks & Mazurek) interviewed 16 professional, primarily senior software developers and surveyed 178 members of the Rust development community. A participant described "a near-vertical learning curve". The borrow checker and the paradigm shift were the biggest learning hurdles, yet developers report that it changes how they think about ownership ("it never leaves you").
- **Rust 2016 survey.** The Rust Blog's "State of Rust Survey 2016" reports that over 1,900 people answered the challenges question, and "in total, 1 in 4 people commented on the learning curve when talking about Rust's challenges."
- **Crichton et al. ("A Grounded Conceptual Model for Ownership Types in Rust", OOPSLA 2023).** In a study of 36 learners, participants usually identified why the borrow checker rejected a program but "could only fix the program in 46% of cases" and "only create a counterexample in 31% of cases." They "generally did not understand what the borrow checker was protecting them from." A permissions-based pedagogy deployed in the Rust Book raised Ownership Inventory scores by 9% (N = 342, d = 0.56).\[82\]\[83\]
- **Zhu et al. (ICSE 2022).** Lifetime computation "is more challenging than applying an ownership rule."\[84\]
- **Relevance.** If `Txn` hooks require users to reason about linear tokens *and* abort paths, expect steep costs. A permissions/"what could go wrong" mental model measurably helps.

### Performance anchors already cited

These are BoC versus Pony (§4), Orleans KTPS (§3), Snapper's 2× (§3), and Singularity's 4–5× IPC advantage (§1).

## Synthesis: Recurring Lessons and Open Problems for a Unified Linear + Transactional + Actor Model

### Lessons

1. **Linearity and transactions answer different questions. Keep them layered, not fused.** Every successful system separates *static exclusivity* (iso/region/exchange-heap ownership) from *dynamic exclusivity acquisition* (cown/`when`, `LockAsync`, 2PL). Verona's `iso` versus `cown[T]` maps directly onto your `T 1` versus `Txn (T 1)`: inside the transaction body the resource should *look like* a `T 1` (Verona: "from the inside, an acquired `cown[T]` looks like a `T`").\[1\]
2. **Optimistic re-execution and linear external resources don't mix.** The failures share a cause. STM.NET hit I/O, Haskell exposes it only through `unsafeIOToSTM` with its warnings, Orleans can only ask for "programmer discipline", and Rust STM can only document "don't have side effects". Systems that touch non-undoable resources atomically go *pessimistic with a declared footprint*: BoC, Durable Functions, Snapper PACTs, Calvin.
3. **Footprint declaration is the price of deadlock freedom and of not rolling back.** BoC, Durable Functions and Snapper all need it. Orleans recovers it heuristically via reconnaissance queries, at the cost of running the logic twice. BoC's top admitted limitation is dynamically growing resource sets.
4. **User-extensible semantics work only with a small, checkable contract.** Boosting needs commutativity plus inverses, open nesting needs abstract locks plus compensations, and Orleans needs ETag-conditional storage. Where the contract is unchecked, users get it wrong or are surprised (Orleans deadlock timeouts). Where it is structurally constrained, guarantees survive (Durable Functions' five critical-section rules).
5. **Distribution kills shared-memory transaction models.** Akka dropped Transactors because they were "not scalable and distributable".\[41\] BoC lists distribution as open. Orleans needed 2PC with early lock release and still pays roughly 10–20× throughput overhead versus plain writes.
6. **Exceptions and abort are where linear disciplines leak.** Sing# didn't check exceptional paths, Exceptional GV exists because "linear session types are unrealistic" without failure handling, and affine dropping re-introduces deadlock.

### Recommendations for `Txn (T 1)`

- **Make the resource's rollback capability part of its type, and dispatch the `Txn` policy on it.** A practical lattice, drawn from the prior art:
  - **Buffered**: pure memory, redo log, can be optimistic and retried (Haskell STM).
  - **Invertible**: operation plus inverse plus abstract lock (boosting).
  - **Compensatable**: commits early, compensates on outer abort (open nesting, sagas).
  - **Irrevocable**: must hold exclusive access pessimistically for the whole transaction and must never be retried (Welc et al.; BoC). This should be most FileHandle writes, unless they are staged.
- **Type the abort path for linear resources.** For example, `Txn r a` should, on abort, yield the original `r` (or a typed "closed" witness) back to the owner. Silent drop must be impossible. No existing system checks this, and it is the most novel contribution available to you.
- **Use BoC-style multi-resource acquisition as the default cross-actor primitive.** Offer optimistic/retry semantics only for Buffered resources. That gives deadlock freedom by construction and avoids the Orleans "surprise deadlock" experience.
- **Expose the hooks you listed (footprint, acquire/validate, rollback meaning, retry policy) as a GADT, but ship a small set of verified instances**, for example Pessimistic-BoC, Optimistic-STM, Boosted and Saga. Treat user-defined instances like boosting specifications that come with obligations: commutativity, and inverse ∘ op = id on the abstract state.
- **Handle dynamic footprints with a two-phase "reconnaissance then acquire" mode** (Orleans/Calvin) rather than by releasing and re-acquiring mid-transaction.
- **Use ownership-aware commit (Agrawal et al.) for nested transactions across actor boundaries.** State owned by the current actor commits at its level, and foreign state propagates up.\[27\] This is the only nesting rule in the literature with a clean serializability theorem.
- **Defer irrevocable IO to commit** via `onCommit` finalizers (Schröder's `atomicallyWithIO`; the STM.NET exploration). The xCalls evidence (33 of 284 syscalls irrevocable) suggests most OS-level effects can be deferred or compensated.\[26\]\[31\]

### Open problems

- A type system that statically guarantees a linear resource is either consumed by commit or returned by abort, across actor boundaries.
- Dynamic footprint growth without rollback, which is BoC's admitted gap.
- Read-shared acquisition modes in BoC-like systems.
- Distributed BoC: co-locating cowns that are frequently acquired together.\[1\]
- Causal-consistent rollback (CauDEr-style) in the presence of irrevocable external effects.

## Caveats

- Several performance figures are micro-benchmarks reported by the systems' own authors. The BoC authors explicitly say realistic gains "would be considerably smaller", and the Orleans KTPS figures use in-memory storage to isolate protocol cost.
- Some Akka rationale comes from mailing-list posts by team members, not formal documentation.
- The HDCC 3.1× figure and the Rust thesis are secondary to the main sources. The Rust thesis is not peer-reviewed.
- Systems listed only as "pointers" (Encore, Kilim, Mezzo, Vale, Austral, Granule, Linear Haskell, Gleam, Inko, Mnesia, Temporal, Ray, CAF, Coyote, Hydro/Bloom, Unison) were not examined in depth. Their absence from the detailed sections reflects limits of this survey's sourcing, not a lack of relevance.
- The claim that no paper types linear resources inside transactions is a negative finding from targeted searches. It may have missed workshop papers.
